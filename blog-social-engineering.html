Social Engineering AI Threats Phishing 2025 8 min read

I almost got phished by an AI. Here's exactly how it happened.

It started with a perfectly worded LinkedIn message from someone I almost recognized. The grammar was flawless. The context was specific. The urgency felt real. This is not a story about being careless — it's about how AI-powered social engineering has gotten so good that the old rules for spotting phishing no longer apply.

By Priyanshi Singhi First-hand account 2026

The message that started it

It was a Tuesday evening. I was wrapping up a lab session on Burp Suite and my LinkedIn had a notification — a connection request plus a message from someone named "Arjun Mehta" who worked at a mid-sized cybersecurity firm I'd actually heard of.

The message — reconstructed from memory

"Hi Priyanshi — I came across your work on adversarial prompt injection through a UPES alumni thread. Your approach to input validation for LLMs is exactly the kind of thinking we need for a project we're running internally. We have a short paid contract (2 weeks, remote) opening up. Would love to share the brief. Are you free for a quick call this week?"

Read that again. There's nothing wrong with it. It references something real — my actual project. It mentions a real context (UPES alumni network). It's complimentary without being over-the-top. The offer is specific and the timeline is concrete. The account had 200+ connections, a professional headshot, and 4 years of posting history.

I accepted the connection and said yes to the call.

What almost got me

The "call" was a Google Meet link. Arjun joined — or what I thought was Arjun. The video was slightly pixelated (he apologized for a weak connection). His voice was clear. He spoke knowledgeably about LLM security, mentioned specific CVEs I recognized, and name-dropped a real researcher in the space whose work I knew.

Then came the ask: "We use an internal research platform. I'll send you a trial access link — just verify your GitHub identity through it so we can set up your permissions."

That was the moment

Everything before it was perfectly engineered setup. The entire 20-minute conversation existed to make that one sentence feel routine rather than suspicious.

I paused. Something felt slightly off about the phrasing — "verify your GitHub identity" is not how legitimate platforms talk. I asked for the company's official domain to check the platform URL against. There was a half-second delay before he gave me one — and when I Googled it, the domain had been registered 3 months ago.

I ended the call, checked the LinkedIn profile more carefully, and found that every post on it was between 18–24 months old. No activity since. Classic dormant account purchased or compromised for use in targeted attacks.

How AI made this attack possible

Here's what I think happened, and what makes this fundamentally different from phishing attempts of 5 years ago:

The scraping was AI-assisted

My project name, my university, my area of focus — all public on LinkedIn and my GitHub. An AI agent scraping public profiles and crafting personalized opening messages is trivially easy today. The message didn't feel templated because it wasn't — it was generated specifically for me.

The voice on the call was likely cloned or synthesized

Real-time voice synthesis has gotten disturbingly good. Tools exist today that can synthesize a natural-sounding voice from minimal training data — and maintain conversation coherence through AI-assisted scripting. The "bad connection" excuse for slightly off video is a classic tell, but it's plausible enough that most people don't question it.

The old checklist doesn't work anymore

Remember the phishing checklist your university's IT department gave you? Let me go through it:

✗
"Look for spelling and grammar mistakes" — There were none. AI writes better English than most humans.
✗
"Be suspicious of unsolicited contact" — LinkedIn messages from professionals are routine. This felt like every other recruiter message I'd received.
✗
"Check if they know personal details about you" — They did. Detailed ones. Scraped from public sources.
✗
"Trust your gut feeling" — My gut felt fine until the very last moment. That's the whole point of the buildup.

What actually saved me

✓
Noticing unnatural phrasing in the technical ask. "Verify your GitHub identity" is not how real platforms phrase authorization flows. Domain-specific language inconsistencies are hard for AI to perfectly emulate.
✓
Domain registration date check. A whois lookup on any domain takes 10 seconds. A 3-month-old domain for a company that claims 5 years of operation is an instant red flag.
✓
Activity gap on the LinkedIn profile. 18 months of silence followed by sudden targeted outreach is a pattern worth looking for. Real professionals post occasionally.
✓
Asking for the company's official domain proactively. A legitimate recruiter will give this without hesitation. A microsecond of delay before answering is meaningful.

The new rules for spotting AI phishing

The old checklist is dead. Here's what actually works in 2025:

  1. Any credential ask, no matter how small, is a stop sign. "Verify your GitHub", "confirm your Google account", "log in to our platform" — treat all of these with the same suspicion as "send me your password." OAuth flows exist. Legitimate platforms use them.
  2. Run whois on every domain before clicking. Registration date, registrar, and expiry date tell you more about a company's legitimacy than its website design.
  3. Check LinkedIn profile activity dates, not just connection counts. Purchased or compromised accounts have follower counts but no recent organic activity.
  4. Establish a code word system for sensitive communications. If you regularly communicate with colleagues about sensitive projects, agree on a rotating verification word that an AI wouldn't know.
  5. For video calls: ask an off-script question early. Something specific and unpredictable — a reference to a mutual contact, a detail about a recent event, something an AI wouldn't have in its training context for this conversation. A real human answers naturally. A script stumbles.
  6. Slow down when something feels flattering. Attackers know that professional validation makes people lower their guard. The more an outreach compliments your specific work, the more scrutiny it should get, not less.
The hardest part to accept

This attack would have fooled most cybersecurity students — and a lot of professionals. That's not a knock on anyone. It's a statement about how much the threat has evolved. Being security-aware is no longer sufficient. You need security habits that activate before your judgment does.

A note on reporting

I reported the LinkedIn profile. It was taken down within 48 hours. I also checked the domain I'd been given — it was serving a fake GitHub OAuth page designed to harvest tokens. I submitted it to PhishTank and Google Safe Browsing. Within a week, both had flagged it.

Reporting matters. These attacks scale because attackers assume their targets won't bother. Spending 5 minutes to report a phishing attempt protects whoever the same infrastructure would have targeted next.

Frequently asked questions

How common are AI-generated spear phishing attacks in 2025?
Significantly more common than most people realize. Security researchers have documented a 60%+ year-over-year increase in targeted AI-assisted phishing attacks since 2023. The barrier to creating personalized, convincing attacks has dropped dramatically with accessible LLM APIs.
Are AI phishing attacks only targeting tech professionals?
No — but tech professionals are disproportionately targeted because their credentials (GitHub, AWS, cloud platforms) have high resale value and can enable further attacks. Finance and HR professionals are also heavily targeted for credential and payment fraud.
What's the best tool to check if a domain is suspicious?
For quick checks: whois.domaintools.com for registration age, VirusTotal for reputation, and urlscan.io for a visual scan of what a URL actually loads. Get comfortable with all three — they take under 2 minutes combined.
Can my organization protect against AI social engineering at scale?
Training alone won't cut it anymore. Organizations need to implement verification protocols for any request involving credentials or access changes — regardless of how trusted the source seems. Out-of-band verification (a phone call to a known number, not one provided in the suspicious message) is the most reliable defense.
