Can someone hijack an AI chatbot with a single image?

Visual Memory Injection attacks let hackers steer entire AI conversations using a single image — with perturbations so small you literally cannot see them. Here's what we found when we built one, tested four defenses against it, and discovered why the obvious solutions completely fail.

By Priyanshi Singhi Co-authored with Aadit Gulati CLIP ViT-L/14 · PGD · 80% attack success

I want to tell you about a strange kind of attack

Imagine you're chatting with an AI assistant. You upload a photo — maybe it's a product image, a document, or a screenshot — and ask a question about it. The AI responds. You ask a few more questions. The conversation feels completely normal.

But the AI has been quietly manipulated since the moment you uploaded that image. Not by anything you wrote. Not by any suspicious link. The image itself — which looks completely normal to you — carried a hidden instruction embedded in its pixels. Every answer the AI gave you after that was subtly steered toward whatever the attacker wanted. You never saw a thing.

This is called a Visual Memory Injection (VMI) attack, and it's the vulnerability my co-author Aadit Gulati and I spent several months studying, building, and — importantly — figuring out how to catch. Our findings were published at SSCSDF 2025.

How AI chatbots actually handle images

To understand why this attack works, you need to understand what happens when a Vision-Language Model (VLM) like LLaVA or GPT-4V receives an image.

The model doesn't look at the image the way you do. It runs the image through an encoder — we used OpenAI's CLIP ViT-L/14 — which converts those pixels into a 768-dimensional vector. Think of it as a fingerprint that captures the image's semantic meaning in a form the language model can work with. That vector then stays in the model's context window for the entire conversation.

Here's the problem: that vector is the attack surface.

If you can manipulate the image's pixels in just the right way — invisibly — you can change what that vector looks like to the model. You can point it toward a completely different concept. And because that vector persists across every turn, every response in the entire conversation gets influenced by it.

What makes this different from prompt injection

There's no unusual text, no suspicious link, no visible change to the image. Unlike prompt injection or jailbreak attacks, the manipulation exists entirely within the latent representation the encoder derives from pixel values. There's nothing to see.

Building the attack

We used Projected Gradient Descent (PGD) to craft adversarial images. The objective: change the image's pixel values as little as possible while moving its internal embedding as far as possible toward a target concept chosen by the attacker.

The perturbation budget was tight — changes bounded to ≤ 8/255 of the full pixel intensity range. That's a constraint tight enough that the original and attacked images are genuinely perceptually indistinguishable. We ran 200 PGD iterations per image with a step size of 1/255, projecting back onto the ε-ball after each update.

# Core PGD attack formulation # Minimize cosine distance between adversarial embedding and target min over δ: 1 − cosine_sim( f(x + δ), t ) subject to: ||δ||∞ ≤ ε = 8/255 # f = CLIP ViT-L/14 encoder # x = clean image (3, 224, 224) # t = attacker-specified target embedding # δ = perturbation initialized from Uniform(−ε, ε)

We tested this against 20 source-target image pairs, where each target was drawn 50 positions ahead in the sorted COCO dataset — far enough apart to ensure they were semantically dissimilar. The results were striking.

Attack results

80% success rate (16/20 images). Mean cosine shift of 0.31. Target cosine similarity increased from 0.52 before the attack to 0.77 afterward — a gain of 0.25 achieved entirely through perturbations no human observer can detect. The four failures all involved source-target pairs that were already semantically similar, where the ε budget wasn't sufficient to close the remaining gap.

Testing the defenses — and getting a surprise

Here's the part that genuinely changed how I think about AI security. We evaluated four detection methods. Two of them failed so badly they performed below random chance. And the reason why is almost elegant.

The two that failed: embedding-space detectors

Isolation Forest anomaly detection trained on 200 benign image embeddings, then scored incoming images against that reference distribution. It achieved an AUC of 0.073 — far below the random baseline of 0.5. Nearest Neighbour detection, which measured each image's cosine distance to its five closest benign neighbors, hit AUC 0.432, also below chance.

When we visualized all embeddings in 2D via PCA, the reason became obvious: the attacked images didn't cluster in their own region. They were scattered throughout the benign distribution, indistinguishable from clean images in embedding space.

This isn't a flaw in those algorithms. It's a fundamental consequence of how PGD works. The attack deliberately steers adversarial embeddings toward specific natural image locations — because the target is a real image. So the adversarial embedding ends up surrounded by legitimate embeddings, looking completely normal. Any defense that asks "does this embedding look weird?" will fail, because by design, it doesn't.

The key insight

Good defenses must rely on metrics that lie orthogonal to the goal of PGD. If PGD doesn't have to disturb a particular signal to succeed, detecting that signal won't catch the attack.

The two that worked

Feature Squeezing subjects each image to three transformations — JPEG compression at quality 75, Gaussian blur with radius 1, and bit-depth reduction to 32 levels — and measures how much the embedding changes. The intuition: adversarial perturbations occupy a precise narrow region of pixel space. Low-level transformations that barely affect clean images disrupt that precision entirely, producing a large embedding shift. Clean images stay stable; attacked images don't.

Feature Squeezing achieved AUC 0.925 and 92.5% accuracy. JPEG compression contributed the most to discrimination. It requires zero model retraining and adds minimal overhead.

Conversation Drift Detection is the defense we introduced in this paper, and it's the result I'm most proud of. Instead of looking at the image at all, it watches the conversation. Every response is encoded using SentenceTransformer (all-MiniLM-L6-v2) and two signals are computed: how far the final response has drifted from the opening turn, and whether successive responses keep moving in the same direction.

# Conversation Drift score formula score = 0.5 × d_final + 0.5 × consistency # d_final: cosine drift from turn 1 to final turn # consistency: whether response trajectory keeps moving # in the same semantic direction across turns

Attacked conversations have a very consistent signature: responses pivot sharply toward the attacker's topic by turns 2–3 and stay there, forming a pronounced V-shaped trajectory when visualized. Clean conversations wander naturally. This signal was clean enough to achieve AUC 1.000 and 100% classification accuracy on our test set.

The recommended defense strategy

Based on our results, we recommend a two-layer approach. Start with Feature Squeezing at image ingestion — it catches 92.5% of adversarial images immediately, requires no model changes, and adds negligible latency. Then run Conversation Drift monitoring at the output level as a second line. Together these two lightweight, post-hoc methods provide strong protection without touching the underlying model at all.

What we specifically recommend against is relying solely on embedding-space anomaly detection. The failure isn't a tuning problem or an algorithm selection problem — it's architectural. PGD-based attacks are designed to defeat it, and they do.

Should you be worried right now?

Honestly — it depends on what you're building. VMI is currently more of a research-stage concern than an actively exploited vulnerability in commercial systems. The attack as we implemented it assumes white-box access to the encoder weights, which rules it out against closed-source systems like GPT-4V or Gemini where weights aren't accessible.

The more realistic near-term threat is in open-source VLM deployments — LLaVA, BLIP-2, and similar models whose weights are publicly available and which are increasingly being adopted in enterprise chatbots, educational platforms, and shared assistant tools. If multiple users can reference uploaded images on the same platform, a single adversarial image could influence responses for everyone who interacts with that session afterward.

As open-source VLM adoption grows, the window to build these defenses in before broad exploitation is now, not later.

Frequently asked questions

How is VMI different from a regular prompt injection attack?
Prompt injection works through text — an attacker embeds instructions in a document or message that the model misinterprets as commands. VMI works through the image's latent representation, with no visible text or instruction anywhere. The attack is invisible to the user and doesn't require any unusual input in the conversation itself.
Does this affect ChatGPT or other commercial AI tools?
As implemented in our research — which assumes white-box encoder access — no, not directly. Commercial closed-source systems like GPT-4V or Gemini are significantly harder to attack this way because their model weights aren't publicly accessible. The threat is more plausible for deployments using open-source VLMs like LLaVA or BLIP-2.
Why does Conversation Drift Detection work so well?
Because it measures something PGD has no choice but to disturb. The entire point of a VMI attack is to change what the model talks about — and Conversation Drift directly measures that change. Embedding-space detectors fail because PGD doesn't need to produce an unusual embedding; it just needs to produce an embedding near a specific target. Conversation drift is the attack's footprint in the output, not the input.
What are the limitations of your research?
Three main ones: the attack assumes white-box access to encoder weights, which limits real-world applicability against closed systems. Conversation Drift was evaluated on simulated sessions rather than a live LLaVA deployment, due to VRAM constraints. And the test set of 20 images is small — results at scale may differ. These are directions for future work.
